# Scene Design Method

Every scene should work locally before it serves the architecture.

## Minimum scene fields

1. **Surface event** — what physically/socially happens?
2. **Character objective** — what does someone want right now?
3. **Emotional function** — what human experience is the scene carrying?
4. **Pressure / relief** — what gets harder, easier, safer, riskier, closer, farther?
5. **Local truth** — what is genuinely true inside this scene?
6. **Relationship change** — what changes between people?
7. **Reader effect** — what should the reader feel/believe here?
8. **Continuity payload** — facts introduced or altered.
9. **Later affordance** — what could this scene support later without becoming planted-clue machinery?
10. **Constraint** — what must this scene not do?

## Scene deletion test

If the scene only explains the philosophy, backstory, or mechanism, delete it or rebuild it as lived action.

If removing the structural layer leaves no interesting human scene, the scene is not ready.

## Anchor scenes

Draft a small number of **anchor scenes** before locking the whole novel. Anchor scenes establish the book's actual voice, emotional resolution, and human reality.

An anchor scene should:

- be intrinsically readable
- contain no synopsis-like explanation
- prove the protagonist can carry prose
- establish sensory and behavioral specificity
- teach the author something the outline did not know

After drafting an anchor scene, planning must be allowed to change in response to the prose.
